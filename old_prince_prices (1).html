18xx.games · bidding companion

What to pay for each private

Every opening-auction lot that drew at least one bid and could be pinned to a player, across every finished game on record. Use it as a price sheet: what a private usually goes for, whether paying up is worth it, which privates pair well, and which railways reward the player who starts them.

How to read the win figures. Everything below is measured as a win rate — how often the player who bought a lot (or the pair, or the company) went on to finish first. Compare it to the fair share: with equal seats, each player wins of the time by luck alone. Above that line is good, below is bad. Green beats fair share, red trails it.

The price sheet

What a private goes for

Sorted by typical price. The bar spans the usual range (the middle 80% of winning bids); the notch is the median — a fair anchor for what to bid — and the hollow tick is face value. Anything to the right of the notch is you paying a premium.

Click a column to sort. Typical is the median winning bid; range is the 10th–90th percentile; win rate is how often the buyer finished first. PEIR mini-shares are dimmed — they mostly go at face and rarely draw a real fight.

Is paying up worth it?

Winners pay a hair less

The gap between what eventual winners paid for a lot and what everyone else paid. Bars point left when winners paid less. The hairline is the 95% confidence interval; a bar whose hairline crosses zero can't be told apart from no effect. The honest read: discipline is worth a couple of dollars a lot, not twenty — so anchor on the median and don't chase.

Does taking the lot help you win?

Win rate by private

How often the buyer of each private finished first, measured against the fair-share line. This is the same question as the price sheet from the other side: not "what does it cost" but "does owning it help." Most privates hug the line; a few lean.

Private coordination

Which privates pair well

Each player leaves the auction holding a handful of privates. This asks whether certain combinations win more than the privates would on their own. Rows and columns are the named privates, ordered by their solo win rate. A cell is the win rate of players who left the auction holding both; the outlined diagonal is the private on its own.

Company coordination

Which railway to start, and when

For each of the seven PEIR railways, how often the player who started it — the president who parred it, or bought its concession — went on to win. The big overall column is the reliable signal; the smaller cells split that by float order, meaning whether the company was the 1st, 2nd, 3rd… to open that game. Position 1 and 2 are always the two railways seeded as the Mainline and Shortline at setup.

For the curious · pooled across 3p and 4p

The same price, a different railway

Eight of the privates carry a share of whichever company is the Mainline or Shortline that game — and which company that is gets drawn at random during setup. So the same private, at the same price, can hand you a share of a strong railway or a weak one. This grid uses a neutral-0.500 finish score instead of a win rate, because that scale is comparable across 3 and 4-player games and lets both pool together: 0.500 is an average finish, 1.000 is always first, 0.000 is always last. The small figure is what the buyer paid.
